Our mission

The Institute in brief

Founded in 2026, the Institute for Saharan Margins (IMS) is an independent think tank. Its work covers models of governance, local conflict-resolution mechanisms, migration dynamics, the uptake of new technologies, the future of agro-pastoral systems under globalisation, environmental and climate issues, regional security and the political economy of resources, with particular attention to the climate-peace-security nexus.

A public-interest organisation, IMS produces strategic studies and foresight analysis across four regional blocs: the Sahel, the Lake Chad basin, the Sahara and the Red Sea. It informs the public, and trains and advises public and private actors in business, institutional and diplomatic circles.

Committed to intellectual independence and analytical rigour, the Institute works with universities, strategic research centres, public decision-makers and international institutions, to inform, raise awareness of and anticipate the geopolitical, economic, climate and environmental challenges of the Saharan margins.

The Institute publishes in French and English.

Policy brief

What do we mean by “the Saharan margins”?

The object the Institute took for its name. This brief sets out its definition and what is at stake.

A zone of transition, not a border

The Saharan margins are the zones of ecological, climatic and socio-spatial transition on the periphery of the Sahara: to the north, the pre-desert steppes; to the south, the Sahelian belt. They are not lines of rupture but porous, shifting zones, cut through by a steep aridity gradient. Rainfall is irregular, ecosystems fragile, and landscapes alternate between steppe, shrub savannah and oasis.

Societies built on movement

Nomadic and semi-nomadic pastoralism dominates, alongside localised subsistence farming. These spaces long lived off their position as trading crossroads on the trans-Saharan routes, and remain places of cultural mixing. Urbanisation is recent and rapid, concentrated around water points and road corridors. It runs up against advancing desertification, extreme water stress and the continuing degradation of soils.

A periphery turned epicentre

Far from centres of power, these margins accumulate governance difficulties, intercommunal tensions — particularly between farmers and herders — and the entrenchment of armed groups and trafficking networks. Spaces of transit and periphery, they have become the epicentre of complex security crises: asymmetric conflicts involving non-state armed groups, military operations, militias. The growing scarcity of agro-pastoral resources aggravates intercommunal conflict. Infrastructural isolation, structural poverty and poor access to basic services hold back the economic integration of these regions.

Political marginalisation

These territories suffer political marginalisation relative to state centres of power. The weak presence of the state sustains instability and calls for responses in terms of decentralisation, representation and the political integration of local populations.

Subsoil wealth as a driver of conflict

The subsoil holds strategic extractive resources: gold, uranium, oil. That wealth often turns against the territories that hold it. Formal extraction brings little benefit to local communities and feeds a sense of injustice; artisanal or illegal extraction, artisanal gold mining in particular, funds armed groups and consolidates a cross-border criminal economy.

Objective 1

Produce evidence

Foresight analysis as decision-support tools: the climate-peace-security nexus, clandestine artisanal gold mining, artisanal antimony and diamond extraction and its environmental consequences, climate mobility, sustainable urbanisation, and the valuing of natural ecosystems.

Objective 2

Bring research and decision-making together

Establish a practical framework for collaboration between universities, strategic research centres, public decision-makers and international institutions, to inform, raise awareness of and anticipate the climate and environmental challenges of the Saharan margins.

Areas covered

Four regional blocs, fourteen countries

A tight perimeter, chosen for its coherence: these blocs share flows of people, weapons and resources that ignore administrative borders.

Research areas

Seven research areas

A broad thematic remit over a deliberately narrow geographical area.

Political regimes & trajectories of power

Structures of power, clan-military realignments, successions and transitions in the states of the region.

Regional security & armed actors

Non-state armed groups, militias, foreign forces, asymmetric conflict and smuggling economies in border areas.

Political economy of resources

Gold, uranium, oil, clandestine artisanal mining, antimony and diamonds: the power relations these resources structure, and the armed-group financing they sustain.

Governance of the margins & conflict resolution

Territorial public policy, decentralisation and political representation, local mechanisms for resolving intercommunal tension.

Climate, environment & agro-pastoral systems

Desertification, water stress, soil degradation, the future of pastoralism under globalisation, and the climate-peace-security nexus that binds them.

Migration, mobility & urbanisation

Migration dynamics, climate mobility, forced displacement, and rapid urbanisation around water points and road corridors.

Strategic studies, technology & foresight

Monitoring, rupture scenarios and the uptake of new technologies, for donors, diplomatic services and companies exposed to the region.

Our method

How we work

Our notes draw on three kinds of material. None is sufficient alone, and we always say where our claims come from.

01

Field network

Contacts based in the region: local administrators, traders, herders, journalists, former combatants. They are the ones who flag a movement before it becomes a press release.

02

Open sources (OSINT)

Satellite imagery, conflict databases, customs and mining registers, UN panel of experts reports, and humanitarian data from UNHCR, OCHA and the IPC.

03

Academic literature

Work in political anthropology, the history of Saharan spaces and the sociology of the state, so that no crisis is read as an event without a past.

Publication rules
  • Uncorroborated field information is never published as fact.
  • Figures are attributed to their source, with its date, rather than repeated as general truths.
  • Confidence levels are stated when they are low.
  • Errors are corrected on the page itself, with the correction noted.
  • The identity of field contacts is protected.
Independence

What guarantees our analytical freedom

An institute writing about authoritarian regimes, war economies and foreign interference cannot afford ambiguity about its funding. We apply three simple rules.

No government in the area covered, no political party and no armed actor funds the Institute, directly or through an intermediary. A private mandate buys an analysis, never a conclusion: the client commissioning a confidential note changes neither its content nor its recommendations. Publications in the open-access collection remain entirely in the Institute's hands, whatever commercial relationships exist elsewhere.

Our funding comes from institutional subscriptions, research grants and advisory work. It is set out in detail on the Support page.

Editorial formats

Four formats, two collections

The Cahiers du Tibesti bring together open-access output. The Notes du Darfour gather confidential work produced for institutional clients.

Policy brief

2 pages · on request

The decision-ready digest of a file: diagnosis, headline figures, options for action. Designed to be read in five minutes before a meeting.
